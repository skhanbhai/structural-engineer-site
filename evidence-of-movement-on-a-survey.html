Overview / Crack inspection / Evidence of movement on a survey
Guide · Written by a chartered structural engineer

What does ‘evidence of movement’ mean on a Level 3 survey?

That the surveyor saw signs the building has moved at some point in its life and could not say from a visual inspection whether that movement has stopped. It is not a finding that the house is unsafe. Most London houses over a hundred years old have moved and most of that movement finished decades ago.

The phrase matters because of what usually follows it: a recommendation to get a structural engineer’s opinion before you exchange. Below is what the common survey phrases actually mean, the four questions an engineer’s report answers and what to do, in order, while the purchase is still on hold.

Section / 01 The short answer

The surveyor is flagging a question, not giving a verdict.

A Level 3 survey is a visual inspection of the whole building. It is very good at spotting signs of movement. It is not designed to work out what caused them or whether they are still happening.

Why did they refer it on?

Deciding whether movement is historic or ongoing usually needs a closer look at the pattern, the ground, the drains and the trees nearby. Sometimes it needs monitoring over months. That is outside what a survey covers, so a careful surveyor recommends a specialist instead of guessing. It protects you as much as them.

Does it mean the house is unsafe?

Almost never. Distorted door frames, sloping floors and old cracks are what a Victorian or Edwardian house looks like after a century on London clay. The survey is telling you the building has a history. The engineer’s job is to tell you whether that history is over.

Historic or ongoing: why it matters

Historic movement that has stopped is usually a cosmetic repair and a sentence in a report. Ongoing movement means there is a live cause to find and deal with, which can affect the price, the insurance and the lender. Almost everything about the decision to buy turns on that one distinction.

Do not exchange until it is answered. Once you exchange contracts you are committed to buy, so a structural question raised by your survey needs answering first. Tell your solicitor about the recommendation straight away. Most sellers and agents expect a few days’ delay for it and a report usually follows about five days after a site visit.
Section / 02 The survey wording

What the common survey phrases actually mean.

These are the lines buyers send us most often, quoted from their surveys, with what each one usually means in practice.

“Evidence of historic movement which appears long-standing”

The reassuring version. The surveyor thinks the movement is old and has probably stopped but has seen enough of it to want that confirmed. This is the most common wording and the one most often signed off by an engineer as historic and stable. Old paint or filler inside the cracks and doors that were re-hung years ago are the kind of signs that support it.

“Movement which may be ongoing”

The one to take more seriously. The surveyor has seen something recent: clean-edged cracks, fresh filler that has cracked again or a pattern that fits a live cause such as a large tree or a leaking drain nearby. It does not mean the purchase is over. It does mean the cause needs identifying and sometimes monitoring before anyone can tell you what it will cost.

“Further investigation by a structural engineer before exchange”

An instruction, not a formality. If you exchange without it and the movement turns out to be live, the cost is yours. It is also a line your lender’s valuer may repeat, in which case the mortgage offer can be held until a report is provided.

Condition rating 3

The survey’s highest rating, not its worst outcome. On an RICS Home Survey a 3 covers defects that are serious or need to be repaired, replaced or investigated urgently. Structural movement often gets a 3 simply because it needs investigating. Read the text under the rating, not just the number.

Sloping or uneven floors

Very common in older houses and usually historic. Timber floors sag over a century and internal walls settle onto them. A slope towards an external wall, a bay or a removed wall below is worth a closer look. A gentle slope across a whole floor of a Victorian house usually is not.

Sticking doors and out-of-square openings

Doors are the house’s own monitoring. A door that was planed or re-hung years ago and has not stuck since points to historic movement. Doors that have only recently started sticking (especially if they jam in summer and free up in winter) point to seasonal ground movement on clay.

Cracking to the bay window

The single most common location we see. Bays usually sit on shallower foundations than the main house, so they move more with the seasons and tend to crack where they join it. Often historic. It becomes more significant if the crack tapers, shows inside and out or the bay is near a tree.

Chimney breast removed, support not seen

A paperwork question as much as a structural one. The surveyor could not see what holds up the masonry above the gap. The seller’s solicitor should be asked for a Building Regulations completion certificate. If there is none, it is usually fixable by regularisation. Our chimney breast page covers that route.

Section / 03 The report

The four questions an engineer’s report should answer.

If a report does not answer these in plain words, it has not done the job your solicitor and lender need it to do.

01

Is it historic or ongoing?

The headline answer. Read from the age of the cracks, the pattern across the whole building and whether any cause is still present. Where the evidence genuinely cannot decide it on one visit, a good report says so and recommends monitoring instead of guessing either way.

The headline
02

What caused it?

Settlement after the house was built, seasonal clay movement, a tree, a leaking drain, a failed lintel, a wall taken out without enough support. The cause decides the repair. It also decides whether it is something buildings insurance would ever be involved in.

Decides the repair
03

Does anything need doing?

Often the answer is repointing and redecoration. Sometimes it is a new lintel, crack stitching or a drain repair. Rarely it is more. The report should say which and, where it can, give a sense of scale so you know what you are negotiating about.

Scale of works
04

What should happen next?

Nothing, a CCTV drain survey, a period of crack monitoring, questions for the seller about past claims or a quote from a contractor. A clear next step is what lets your solicitor and lender move.

Your next move
Section / 04 Before exchange

What to do, in order, while the purchase is on hold.

Start with the free steps. Most of these questions get answered before anyone needs to visit.

1. Tell your solicitor and hold the exchange

Forward the relevant survey section. Your solicitor can tell the seller’s side that a structural opinion is being obtained, which is a normal request and rarely a surprise to the agent.

2. Ask the seller four questions

Has there ever been a subsidence or heave claim on the property? Are there any previous engineer’s reports or monitoring results? Was the house ever underpinned and is there a guarantee? Is there a completion certificate for any walls or chimney breasts taken out? The answers often settle half the question.

5. Use the report to decide

Proceed as planned, renegotiate on the cost of the works, agree a retention or a period of monitoring or walk away. Each is a reasonable outcome. The point is that you choose with the answer in front of you and not after you are committed.

If you are the seller

A buyer’s survey flagging movement is the most common reason a London sale stalls on structure. A signed report explaining that the movement is historic is often cheaper than the price reduction a nervous buyer asks for. It can be shared with the next buyer if this one walks.

Section / 05 FAQ

Survey and movement FAQs.

If your survey says something not covered here, send us the wording. We read every enquiry.

What does ‘evidence of movement’ mean on a survey?

It means the surveyor saw signs that the building has moved at some point, such as cracking, sloping floors or distorted door frames. They could not tell from a visual inspection whether that movement has stopped. It is not a finding that the house is unsafe. Most older London houses show some historic movement. The usual next step is a structural engineer’s opinion on whether the movement is historic or ongoing.

Should I still buy a house with evidence of movement?

Often, yes. Many houses flagged for movement turn out to have historic, stable movement that needs nothing more than repointing and redecoration. The decision should be made with an engineer’s answer in front of you and before you exchange. If the movement is ongoing, the report will say what is causing it and roughly what dealing with it involves, which you can use to renegotiate or decide not to proceed.

Do I need a structural engineer before exchange?

If your survey recommends one, yes. Once you exchange contracts you are committed to the purchase, so any cost that comes from ongoing movement becomes yours. Your lender’s valuer may also ask for a structural engineer’s report before releasing the mortgage offer. Tell your solicitor about the recommendation as soon as you receive the survey.

What is the difference between historic and ongoing movement?

Historic movement happened in the past and has stopped. Typical examples are settlement after the house was built and movement from a tree that was removed long ago. Ongoing movement is still happening because its cause is still present, such as a leaking drain or a tree drawing moisture from clay soil. Old paint or filler inside a crack suggests historic movement. Clean, sharp crack edges and doors that have only recently started sticking suggest ongoing movement.

What does condition rating 3 mean on an RICS survey?

On an RICS Home Survey a condition rating 3 covers defects that are serious or need to be repaired, replaced or investigated urgently. Structural movement is often given a 3 because it needs investigating, not because it has been found to be serious. The text under the rating explains which applies.

How long does a structural engineer’s report take?

With us, a free first review of the survey pages and photos comes back within 1 working day. If a site visit is needed, the signed report usually follows about five days after the visit. Tell us your target exchange date when you first get in touch so the visit can be booked around it.

How much does a structural inspection cost when buying a house?

Our site inspections are a fixed fee from £750 + VAT, confirmed before booking. The first review of your survey pages and photos is free. In many cases it shows a visit is not needed.

Can the seller provide a structural report instead?

A seller’s existing report or monitoring results are worth asking for and can settle a lot. Check how old it is, who it was written for and whether it covers the movement your survey describes. A report commissioned by the seller was written for the seller, so many buyers and lenders still want their own opinion where the survey has recommended one.
