Overview / Crack inspection / Home insurance and cracked walls
Guide · Written by a chartered structural engineer

Does home insurance cover cracked walls?

Usually not the cracks themselves. A buildings policy covers damage caused by an insured event — subsidence, heave, landslip, impact or escape of water. It does not cover cracking from plaster shrinkage, thermal movement, normal settlement or general wear, which is what most cracks turn out to be.

That single distinction decides almost every claim. Below is what is typically covered, what is typically excluded, what actually happens if you notify your insurer, and the cases where paying for an independent engineer is worth it — and the cases where it is money you do not need to spend.

Section / 01 The short version

Covered, not covered, and the grey area.

Insurers do not ask “is there a crack?” They ask “what caused it, and is that cause listed in the policy?”

Usually covered

Cracking caused by subsidence, heave or landslip is a standard peril on most UK buildings policies. So is cracking from an insured event such as impact from a vehicle, a fallen tree, a burst pipe washing out ground beneath a foundation, or storm damage.

Usually excluded

Plaster shrinkage, thermal movement, the normal settlement of a new build or a recent extension, gradual deterioration, poor workmanship and defective design are almost always excluded. Many policies name “settlement” as an exclusion in the same sentence that grants subsidence cover.

The grey area

Cracking at the junction between a house and a newer extension, or after a very dry summer, can be argued either way. This is where the cause has to be established properly rather than guessed at — and where a claim is won or lost.

Read your own policy wording before anything else. Cover varies between insurers and between products from the same insurer. The schedule will name the perils, the exclusions and your excess. Subsidence normally carries a much higher excess than the rest of the policy — £1,000 is the common figure, and some policies set it higher.
Section / 02 The causes

What each cause means for a claim.

The same crack, on the same wall, is claimable or not depending entirely on which of these produced it.

Subsidence

Normally covered. The ground beneath the foundation moves downwards — typically clay shrinking in a dry spell, tree roots drawing moisture out, or a leaking drain washing soil away. This is the classic claimable cause, and the reason subsidence cover exists as a named peril at all.

Subsidence or settlement? →

Heave

Normally covered. The opposite problem: ground swelling upwards, often clay rehydrating after a large tree has been removed. Heave cracking frequently appears months or years after the tree came down, which is why the connection gets missed.

Escape of water

Normally covered. A leaking drain or supply pipe can soften or wash out the ground under a footing. The claim may be handled under escape of water rather than subsidence, sometimes with a lower excess. Worth checking which route your insurer is using.

Impact and storm

Normally covered. A vehicle striking the building, a tree coming down onto it, or storm damage that opens up a wall are all insured events. Cause here is usually obvious and rarely disputed.

Shrinkage and thermal movement

Not covered. New plaster drying out, render cracking as it heats and cools, hairline cracks along ceiling and wall junctions. This is normal building behaviour rather than damage, and every policy excludes it. It is also the single most common thing we are sent photos of.

Common causes of cracks →

Settlement

Not covered. A new building, extension or loft conversion beds down under its own weight in its first few years. It is expected, it slows and it stops. Insurers exclude it by name precisely because it is predictable rather than accidental.

Poor workmanship or design

Not covered by your buildings policy. An undersized lintel, a beam without proper bearing, foundations that were never deep enough. The route here is the contractor, their insurance, or a structural warranty such as NHBC — not a household claim.

Wear, tear and lack of maintenance

Not covered. Blocked gutters saturating a wall for years, perished pointing, a slow leak nobody dealt with. Insurers treat gradual damage as a maintenance failure. The distinction they apply is sudden and accidental versus slow and foreseeable.

Section / 03 The process

What actually happens if you claim.

Worth knowing before you pick up the phone, because a subsidence claim is a long process rather than a quick payout.

1. You notify the insurer

Do this promptly if you genuinely suspect subsidence — most policies require it. Notifying is not the same as claiming, and insurers are used to enquiries that come to nothing. Describe what you have seen and when, and keep dated photographs.

2. They appoint their own experts

Typically a loss adjuster, and usually a structural engineer or subsidence specialist instructed by them. Their investigation — trial pits, drain surveys, root identification — is paid for by the insurer, not by you.

3. Monitoring, often for months

Crack width gauges or level monitoring are usually installed and read over six to twelve months, sometimes longer, because movement has to be shown across a full seasonal cycle. This is the stage homeowners find hardest, and it cannot be shortcut.

4. Cause established, then repair

If subsidence is confirmed, the fix is usually removing or managing the cause — tree works, drain repairs — followed by making good. Underpinning is far less common than people expect and is generally a last resort.

The honest downside. A recorded subsidence claim has to be disclosed when you renew, when you switch insurer and when you sell. It can narrow your choice of insurer, raise your premium and come up in a buyer’s enquiries for years. For borderline cracking with a modest repair cost, that is a real consideration against a £1,000 excess. It is your call, but you should make it knowing both sides.

Section / 06 FAQ

Insurance and cracking FAQs.

General guidance only — your own policy wording decides your cover.

Does home insurance cover cracked walls?

Only where an insured event caused them. Buildings insurance covers cracking that results from subsidence, heave, landslip, impact, storm or escape of water. It does not cover cracking from plaster shrinkage, thermal movement, the normal settlement of a new building or extension, poor workmanship or lack of maintenance. Most cracks homeowners worry about fall into the excluded group.

Is subsidence covered as standard?

On most UK buildings policies, yes, but with a separate and much higher excess than the rest of the policy — commonly £1,000. Cover can be restricted or excluded if the property has a history of subsidence, so check your schedule rather than assuming. The Association of British Insurers publishes general guidance on how the industry handles it.

Will claiming push my premium up?

Very likely, and the effect lasts. A recorded subsidence claim must be disclosed at renewal, when switching insurer and when you sell the property. It can reduce the number of insurers willing to quote. For small repairs close to the excess, that trade-off is worth weighing before you notify.

Should I get my own engineer before I claim?

It is often the cheapest useful step, because it tells you whether a claim is even plausible before you put one on your record. Once the insurer has accepted a claim and appointed their own engineer, a second independent report on the same question is usually unnecessary — their investigation is at their cost, not yours.

Are cracks in a new extension covered?

Usually not. Cracking at the junction between an existing house and a new extension is normally differential settlement, which policies exclude by name. If the cracking is wide, growing or opening at an angle, the question shifts to whether the foundations were adequate — which is a matter for the builder, their insurer or a structural warranty rather than your household policy.

My claim was rejected. What now?

Ask for the insurer’s engineering report and the specific policy clause relied on. If you believe the cause has been misattributed, an independent chartered engineer’s report is the evidence to challenge it with. If the insurer maintains its position, you can escalate through their complaints procedure and then to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which is free to consumers.

Do I need a report for my solicitor or lender?

Frequently, where a homebuyer survey has flagged cracking or movement. Reports are signed by a chartered engineer of the Institution of Structural Engineers and written in a format solicitors, insurers and mortgage lenders accept. The fee is fixed and confirmed before booking.

How do I know whether it is subsidence?

Nobody can confirm subsidence from a photograph alone — it takes monitoring across a seasonal cycle, and often trial pits and a drain survey. Photographs can, however, rule it out fairly reliably in the majority of cases, which is usually the answer people are hoping for. Our separate guide sets out the signs that distinguish the two.
