Overview / Crack inspection / New plaster cracking
Guide · Written by a chartered structural engineer

Is it normal for new plaster to crack?

Usually, yes. Plaster loses water as it dries and shrinks as it does so. If it shrinks faster than it can accommodate, it cracks. Hairline cracks in the weeks and months after a skim are so routine that most plasterers expect to be called back for a few.

The small number of plaster cracks that mean something else look different, and the differences are readable without any equipment. Below is what normal plaster cracking looks like, why it happens, how to tell it from a crack in the wall behind, and what to do about each — which for most people is nothing, for a while.

Section / 01 The short answer

Plaster is the messenger, not the message.

A skim coat is a few millimetres of brittle material with almost no ability to stretch. It cracks at movements far smaller than anything that would trouble the wall it sits on.

Is it normal?

Yes, within limits. Fine cracks under about 1mm, appearing in the first year, in the places listed below, are the plaster settling into its new job. They are a decorating item, not a defect in the building, and usually not a defect in the plastering either.

When do they appear?

Two waves. The first within days to a few weeks, as the skim gives up its water. The second through the first heating season, when the room dries properly for the first time and the timber behind the boards shrinks. After that, new cracks should stop.

Is it the plaster or the wall?

Plaster cracks are fine, follow straight lines or spread as a network, and stay put once they have formed. Cracks in the wall behind tend to run diagonally, widen along their length, reopen after filling and sometimes show on the other side of the wall. Those are the ones to photograph.

The single most useful thing you can do is wait. Do not fill a crack in new plaster in its first few weeks; it is still moving and the filler will crack with it. Mark the ends in pencil, date it, and look again after the first winter with the heating on. Cracks that have not grown by then are finished, and you can decorate over them once and be done.
Section / 02 The patterns

Where new plaster cracks, and what each place means.

Seven of these eight are the plaster talking about itself. Read the last one carefully.

Crazing across the surface

Cosmetic. A network of very fine cracks like the glaze on old pottery, often only visible in low light or after the first coat of paint. The surface dried faster than the material beneath it, usually because heating, sun or a dehumidifier was pushed too hard. It is a finish problem, not a wall problem.

Straight lines at regular spacing

Cosmetic. Dead straight hairlines running vertically at roughly 1.2m intervals, or horizontally at ceiling height, are following the edges of the plasterboard sheets underneath. Either the joints were not scrim-taped or a board has moved slightly on its fixings. Cut out, tape and refill.

Along the wall-to-ceiling line

Cosmetic, and almost universal. The ceiling and the wall are fixed to different things and move differently. Roof timbers shrink through the first winter and the corner opens a fraction. Many decorators simply run a bead of flexible filler along it and never think about it again.

Where new plaster meets old

Cosmetic. A patch or a partial re-skim shrinks; the old plaster around it has finished shrinking years ago. A hairline forms exactly on the boundary between them. The same thing happens where a gypsum skim meets old lime plaster, which behaves quite differently under it.

Internal corners and window reveals

Cosmetic. Two planes of plaster meeting at a corner are both shrinking towards their own centres, so the corner is where they pull apart. Vertical hairlines in internal corners and around window reveals are shrinkage in nearly every case.

Over pipe chases and cable runs

Cosmetic. A channel cut into the wall for a cable or pipe is filled with bonding and skimmed over. The fill is thicker than the skim around it and dries at a different rate, so a faint line traces the route of the chase. Common after a rewire or a new bathroom.

A new extension, in its first two years

Usually settlement, and expected. A new structure beds down on its foundations, and the plaster records it as fine cracks that appear, stop, and do not come back. The tell is that they stabilise. Cracks that keep lengthening or widening past the first two years are a different conversation.

Diagonal, from the corner of an opening

This is the one to look at. A crack that leaves the top corner of a door or window at roughly 45 degrees, is wider at one end than the other, and is more than a hairline, is not shrinkage. It is the wall moving and the plaster reporting it. Photograph it with a coin for scale and check the same spot on the other side of the wall.

Section / 03 Why it happens

Six reasons plaster cracks. Five of them are the plaster.

Knowing the mechanism is what lets you stop worrying about the first five.

01

Drying shrinkage

Gypsum skim goes on wet and loses most of that water within a week or two. Everything shrinks a little as it dries, and plaster cannot stretch to take up the difference, so it relieves the strain by cracking. This is built into the material and no plasterer can fully prevent it.

Inevitable
02

Dried too fast

Heating turned up to speed the job, a south-facing window in July, a dehumidifier running in a closed room. The surface skins over while the body of the plaster is still wet, and the two shrink out of step. That is what produces crazing. Slow, even drying with windows cracked open is what plaster wants.

Avoidable
03

Different backgrounds

Plasterboard, bonding coat, old lime, brick and blockwork all pull water out of a skim at different rates. Where one background meets another, the skim dries unevenly across the line and cracks along it. Most houses that have been altered have several backgrounds in one wall.

Suction
04

Timber drying out

New studs, joists and rafters arrive with moisture in them and shrink across the grain through their first heated winter. The boards fixed to them move a fraction, and the skim on the boards shows it at the joints and corners. This is why the second wave of cracks arrives with the cold weather.

First winter
05

Settlement of new work

A new extension or a rebuilt wall compresses the ground beneath it and beds down over its first couple of years. The movement is tiny and it stops. Fine cracks that appear in that period and then never change are the normal record of it, and insurers exclude them by name because of how normal they are.

Stops on its own
06

Movement in the wall behind

The only one on this list that is not about plaster. A lintel deflecting, a wall moving around an opening, foundation movement below. The plaster cracks because the masonry it is stuck to has cracked, and it will keep cracking until the cause is dealt with, however many times it is filled. Our crack inspection guide covers what those cracks look like.

Worth a look
Section / 04 Next steps

What to do about a crack in new plaster.

Four steps, cheapest first. Most people never need the last one.

1. Leave it through a heating season

Plaster needs to be fully dry before it is painted with anything other than a thinned mist coat, and it needs a winter with the heating on before it has finished moving. Filling and repainting in the first weeks is how people end up doing the job three times.

2. Read the width and the direction

Under 1mm, straight or networked, and in one of the seven places above: cosmetic. The BRE damage scale used across the industry puts anything up to 1mm in its lowest categories, described as fine cracks easily dealt with in normal decoration. Wider than about 3mm, diagonal, or tapering, and you have left the plaster question behind.

3. Fix the cosmetic ones properly

Board-joint lines want cutting back, scrim tape and a fresh fill, or they return. Corners and the ceiling line take a flexible filler. Extensive crazing may need a stabilising coat or a re-skim rather than filler, which will not hold in a network. A hairline that has stopped moving can simply be filled, sanded and painted once.

4. Send a photo of the one that does not fit

A diagonal crack from an opening, anything you can fit a coin into, a crack that has come back after filling, or one you can find on the outside of the same wall. A chartered engineer can usually tell you from photographs whether it is worth doing anything about, and in most cases the answer is still no.

Section / 05 FAQ

New plaster cracking FAQs.

If yours is not here, send it with a photo. We read every enquiry.

Is it normal for new plaster to crack?

Yes. Plaster shrinks as it dries and cannot stretch, so fine hairline cracks in the first weeks and through the first heating season are normal and expected. They typically appear along plasterboard joints, at internal corners, along the wall-to-ceiling line and where new plaster meets old. Cracks under about 1mm that stop growing are a decorating job, not a fault in the building.

How long after plastering do cracks appear?

Usually in two waves. The first comes within days to a few weeks as the skim dries out. The second arrives during the first winter with the heating on, when the room dries fully and any new timber behind the plaster shrinks. New cracks appearing after the first year, or existing ones still growing, are no longer explained by drying and are worth a closer look.

Why is my new plaster cracking in straight lines?

Dead straight cracks at regular spacing are following the edges of the plasterboard underneath. Boards are 1.2m wide, so vertical lines at roughly that interval, or a horizontal line at ceiling height, are board joints. The joints were either not reinforced with scrim tape or a board has moved slightly on its fixings. The repair is to cut the line back, tape it and refill; filler alone usually reopens.

What is crazing in plaster?

Crazing, sometimes called map cracking, is a network of very fine surface cracks resembling the glaze on old pottery. It happens when the surface of the plaster dries and hardens faster than the material beneath it, usually because heating, direct sun or a dehumidifier forced the drying. It is cosmetic and does not indicate anything about the wall. Extensive crazing is better dealt with by a stabilising coat or re-skim than by filling each line.

Should I fill cracks in new plaster straight away?

No. In its first few weeks the plaster is still drying and moving, and filler applied then will crack with it. Wait until the plaster is fully dry and, ideally, until it has been through one winter with the heating on. Mark the ends of any crack in pencil and date it; if it has not grown by then, fill it once, sand and decorate, and it should not come back.

Is cracking in new plaster the plasterer's fault?

Usually not. Drying shrinkage is a property of the material and some hairline cracking is expected on any job. Cracking that does point to workmanship is straight lines at board joints where scrim tape was missed, or crazing where the job was dried out too fast, and most plasterers will return to make good on those. Diagonal cracks or cracks that keep reopening are not a plastering issue at all; they reflect movement in the wall behind.

When is a crack in plaster a sign of a structural problem?

When it stops behaving like shrinkage. The signs are a crack running diagonally, typically from the corner of a door or window; a width above about 3mm, roughly a 10p coin edge; a crack that is noticeably wider at one end than the other; one that has reopened after being filled; or one you can also find on the other side of the same wall. Any one of those is worth a photograph and a free opinion before anything else.
