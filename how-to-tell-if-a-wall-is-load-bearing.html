Overview / RSJ & beam calculations / Is this wall load-bearing?
Guide · Written by a chartered structural engineer

How can you tell if a wall is load-bearing?

Look at what it is holding up. If floor joists land on it, if another wall sits directly on top of it or if it is an outside wall, treat it as load-bearing. If the joists run alongside it and nothing stands above it, it may well not be. You can usually get to a strong suspicion in twenty minutes with a torch and a loft hatch. You cannot get to certainty without seeing inside the floor.

That gap between a suspicion and a certainty is the reason this question matters. Below are the clues an engineer checks first, the drawing that explains most of them, the things nobody can see from the room and what happens next in either case. If you have just bought a house and want to open up the ground floor in the new year, now is the right time to find out.

Section / 01 What it means

A wall is load-bearing if something above would drop without it.

That something is usually a floor, a roof or another wall. The question is never what the wall is made of. It is what the wall is doing.

What does it carry?

Floor joists, roof timbers, the wall above it or a combination. Weight travels down through the building by the most direct route to the ground. A load-bearing wall is one of the stops on that route. Take it out and the load needs a new route, which is what a steel beam provides.

Can it do more than carry?

Yes. Some walls carry very little weight but stop the building from racking sideways, the way a cross brace stiffens a shelf unit. Take out enough of them and the house becomes floppy in the wind. This is called lateral stability and it is the part most often missed.

Is it all or nothing?

No. A wall can carry a whole floor, the end of a single joist or one strut from the roof. That changes the size of the beam and sometimes whether you need one at all. It is why two knock-throughs in identical-looking houses can need quite different steel.

Hollow does not mean safe. The knock test (tapping the wall and listening for a hollow sound) tells you whether it is timber stud or masonry. It does not tell you whether it is carrying anything. Victorian and Edwardian houses are full of timber stud walls that hold up floors, sometimes with the joists of the room above landing on the timber head plate. The sound is a clue about construction, not about load.
Section / 02 The clues

Eight things to check before you ask anyone.

None needs tools beyond a torch, a tape measure and access to the loft. Roughly in the order we check them on site.

1. Which way the joists run

The single most useful clue. Floorboards run at right angles to the joists under them, so look at the boards in the room above. If the boards run parallel to your wall, the joists are crossing it and may be landing on it. If the boards run across the wall, the joists run alongside it. Lines of nails in the boards mark where each joist sits. Carpet hides this, so try a cupboard or the edge of the room where a board can be lifted.

2. What stands directly above it

Go upstairs and stand over it. If a wall on the floor above is in the same line as the one you want to remove, the load from that wall comes down through yours. This is the stacking in the third panel of the drawing and it is the clue people most often skip. A wall above that is offset by half a metre still matters. It may be sitting on the floor joists instead, which then need checking too.

3. What is in the loft

Follow the roof down. In the loft, look for timber struts running down from the roof slopes to a beam or to the top of a wall. Look for a binder (a timber running across the ceiling joists) or a hanger tied up to the roof. Look for a water tank. Wherever any of these lands on a wall below, that wall is carrying part of the roof. On the top floor this is the main clue. On the ground floor it adds to the others.

4. Whether it is an outside wall

Always assume yes. Front, back and side walls carry the floors, the roof and the wind, so any new opening in them needs a beam or lintel. That includes the back wall when you build an extension and the side wall in a side return. A wall that used to be outside and is now inside, because an extension was built against it, is almost always still structural.

5. Whether it is the spine wall

The kitchen-diner wall in most terraces. In a typical London terraced or semi-detached house a wall runs from front to back down the middle, between the hallway and the rooms. Floor joists often span from the party wall onto this spine wall. Most kitchen and dining knock-throughs are through the wall that crosses the house instead. Both are frequently structural, for different reasons.

6. How thick it is and what it is made of

A weaker clue than people expect. A brick wall 225mm or more thick (about the length of a brick) is more likely to be structural than a 100mm stud wall. But thin walls can carry load and thick ones can be leftovers that no longer do. Measure at a doorway where you can see the full thickness, including plaster. Use it to back up the other clues, not to replace them.

7. Whether a chimney breast is involved

Its own category. A chimney breast carries the brickwork of the flues above it and often the stack through the roof. Removing one downstairs while the breast upstairs stays put leaves that masonry hanging. Gallows brackets or a beam then have to carry it. The rules for that are covered on our chimney breast removal page.

8. What has already been changed

Previous owners count. A wall that was fine to remove in 1890 terms may now be carrying a loft conversion, a dormer or a bathroom put in during the 1980s. Look for a newer ceiling, a boxed-in beam or a bulkhead in the room above. Any of those suggests the loads were rearranged at some point. Old Building Control certificates in your purchase pack can tell you what was done.

Section / 03 Can’t see it

Six things nobody can tell from the room.

This is where a suspicion stops and a calculation starts. It is also why a builder saying “that one’s fine” is not the same as knowing.

01

Where the joists actually bear

Boards tell you direction. They do not tell you whether a joist stops on the wall or runs over it and carries on. A joist running over the top may still lean on the wall in the middle of its span. Lifting one board over the line of the wall settles it.

Lift one board
02

Point loads from above

A single roof strut, an old beam end or the post of a later alteration can bring a concentrated load onto one spot of a wall. It may be invisible behind plaster on the floor above. It changes what the new beam and its supports have to take.

Behind the plaster
03

What the beam ends sit on

Once a wall comes out, its load moves to the two ends of the new beam. Whatever is under those ends (a pier, a party wall, a footing) now takes a concentrated load it never had. Whether it can is half of every beam calculation.

Padstones and piers
04

The foundation below

Internal walls in older London houses often sit on a shallow brick footing or almost nothing. That is fine while the load is spread along the wall. It may not be once the same load arrives at one point. Sometimes a trial hole is needed.

Under the floor
05

How much bracing is left

Lateral stability depends on how many walls remain across the house, not on any one of them. Removing one may be fine. Removing it after the previous owner took out two others may not be. Only the whole floor plan answers that.

Whole-house view
06

Fire and escape routes

Not structural but it catches people out. In a house with a loft conversion the hallway walls often form the protected escape route from the top floor. Taking one out can need Building Regulations approval even when it carries no weight at all.

Section / 04 Next steps

What happens next, either way.

Start with the cheapest step. Most people can get from “I wonder” to a clear answer in a day, without anyone visiting.

1. Photograph the evidence

The wall from both sides, the floorboards or ceiling in the room above, the same spot from the floor above and a few shots of the loft if you can get up there. Add a rough sketch of the floor plan with the wall marked. That is most of what an engineer needs to give a first view.

2. Get a free first view

Send the photos to a chartered engineer. If the wall is clearly a partition with nothing above it we will tell you so and that is the end of it. If it is clearly structural or uncertain, we will say what the calculations would need to cover before you commit to anything.

3. If it is load-bearing: calculations, then Building Control

The engineer sizes the beam, the padstones under its ends and any temporary propping. Those calculations go to Building Control (either the council or an approved inspector) before work starts. Most builders will not quote properly without them. Design is a fixed fee quoted before any work starts. For a straightforward project allow about 3–4 weeks for the calculations and drawings. It can be sooner, depending on how complex the job is and what information is available.

How beam calculations work →

4. If it touches a party wall: notice first

Where the new beam bears into a wall you share with a neighbour (common in terraces when the spine wall comes out), the Party Wall etc. Act 1996 usually applies. Notice goes to the neighbour before the work, typically at least two months ahead for that kind of job. Building it into your timetable now avoids a delay in spring.

Section / 05 FAQ

Load-bearing wall FAQs.

If yours is not here, send it with a photo. We read every enquiry.

How can I tell if a wall is load-bearing?

Check which way the floor joists run (floorboards run at right angles to them), whether another wall stands directly above it, whether anything in the loft such as a strut, binder or water tank comes down onto it and whether it is an outside wall or the spine wall of a terrace. Joists landing on it, a wall above it or roof timbers bearing on it all mean it should be treated as load-bearing until an engineer has checked.

Can a stud wall be load-bearing?

Yes. Many Victorian and Edwardian houses have timber stud walls that carry floor joists or part of the roof. A hollow sound when you knock on a wall tells you it is timber and plasterboard or lath and plaster. It does not tell you whether it is carrying anything. Joist direction and what sits above it are better clues.

Are all brick internal walls load-bearing?

No, though in older houses many are. Some brick walls were built as partitions or are left over from an earlier layout and no longer carry much. Brick makes a wall more likely to be structural but it is the joists, the walls above and the roof that decide it.

Do I need Building Regulations approval to remove a non-load-bearing wall?

Usually not, if it genuinely carries nothing and plays no part in fire safety. The exception that catches people out is a house with a loft conversion or three or more storeys, where hallway walls often form the protected escape route. Removing one of those can need approval even though it is not structural. If in doubt, ask Building Control before the wall comes down.

Can a builder tell me if a wall is load-bearing?

An experienced builder can often make a good guess and is frequently right. But Building Control will want calculations from a structural engineer for any load-bearing wall removal and the beam size, padstones and propping all come from those calculations. A guess is a sensible starting point. It is not something to take the wall out on.

Can you tell if a wall is load-bearing from photos?

Often enough to give a clear first view. Photos of the wall from both sides, the floor above, the loft and a rough floor plan let an engineer say whether it is clearly a partition, clearly structural or uncertain. Where it is uncertain, lifting one floorboard over the wall usually settles it. Beam calculations themselves can often be prepared from photos, measurements and plans without a visit.

What happens if a load-bearing wall is removed without support?

Usually not a collapse but a slow sag. The floor above deflects, cracks open in the ceiling and in the walls upstairs and doors start to stick. It also leaves the house without a record of the work, which tends to surface when it is sold. A removal with no paperwork can be put right through a regularisation application backed by an engineer’s assessment. It is still cheaper to do it properly the first time.
