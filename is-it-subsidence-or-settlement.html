Overview / Crack inspection / Subsidence or settlement
Guide · Written by a chartered structural engineer

Is it subsidence, or just settlement?

Almost always settlement, or something more ordinary still. Settlement is a building bedding down under its own weight — mostly in its first few years, and it stops. Subsidence is the ground beneath the foundation moving away from under it, and that does not stop on its own.

The two produce different crack patterns, and the differences are readable without any equipment. Below are the signs that separate them, why London ground makes the question more common here than almost anywhere else in the country, and how subsidence is actually confirmed — which is not from a photograph, whatever anyone tells you.

Section / 01 The difference

Three words that get used interchangeably, and should not be.

They describe three different ground behaviours with three different outcomes. Getting the word right changes what happens next, and who pays for it.

Settlement

The building compresses the ground beneath it under its own weight. Normal, expected, and largely finished within the first few years of a new build or extension. It decelerates and stops. Insurers exclude it by name for exactly that reason.

Subsidence

The ground itself moves downwards and takes the foundation with it — clay shrinking in a dry spell, tree roots drawing out moisture, or a leaking drain washing soil away. It does not self-correct, and it is a standard insured peril.

Heave

The opposite: ground swelling and pushing upwards, usually clay rehydrating after a large tree is felled. Cracks often appear months or years after the tree came down, which is why the connection so often gets missed.

The single most useful test costs nothing. Pencil a line across each end of the crack, date it, photograph it, and look again in three months and again in six. Settlement cracks stay put. Movement that is still active will have moved past your marks. No engineer can give you that answer in a single visit, and time is the one instrument you already own.
Section / 02 The signs

What separates one from the other.

No single sign is conclusive. Several together start to mean something, and that is how any engineer reads a wall.

Width

Under 1mm rarely matters. Hairline cracking is a decorating job. Cracks you can fit a 10p coin into, roughly 3mm and above, are the ones worth measuring properly. Above about 5mm, and certainly above 15mm, the conversation changes regardless of cause.

Taper

The most telling single feature. Subsidence cracks are typically wider at the top than the bottom, because one part of the building has rotated downwards. Heave cracks tend to be wider at the bottom. Shrinkage cracks are usually the same width along their length.

Direction

Diagonal is the one to look at. Cracks running at roughly 45 degrees, often stepping through mortar joints in brickwork, indicate one part of a wall moving relative to another. Vertical cracks at a corner or along a plasterboard joint are far more often shrinkage or thermal movement.

Visible from both sides

A useful escalation. A crack you can see internally and externally in the same place has gone through the full thickness of the wall. That is structural rather than decorative, and it moves the enquiry up a level.

Around doors and windows

Openings are where a wall is weakest. Movement concentrates at corners of openings, so cracks radiating diagonally from window and door corners are a classic pattern. Doors and windows that suddenly stick or stop latching are the same story told a different way.

Timing

Late summer is the giveaway. Clay shrinkage cracking typically appears or worsens at the end of a dry summer and partially closes again over a wet winter. Cracking that follows that seasonal rhythm points at ground moisture. Cracking that appeared once and never changed does not.

Location on the building

Junctions and bays first. Cracking at the join between a house and a later extension, or around a bay window, is extremely common and usually differential settlement rather than subsidence — the two parts sit on different foundations built at different times and behave differently.

Recurrence

Filled twice and back again matters. A crack that has been made good and has reopened in the same line is telling you the movement has not finished. That is worth a proper look even when the crack itself is narrow.

Section / 03 Why London

Why the question comes up so often here.

London sits on some of the most subsidence-prone ground in the country, and its housing stock was built to suit an older climate.

01

London Clay

Much of the capital sits on clay that shrinks as it dries and swells as it wets. That seasonal movement is the underlying mechanism behind most genuine subsidence claims in the South East. Geology is mapped nationally by the British Geological Survey.

Shrink-swell ground
02

Thirsty street trees

London planes, poplars, willows and oaks draw large volumes of water out of clay in summer. A mature tree within roughly its own height of the building is the first thing anyone investigating will look at, and street trees belong to the local authority rather than to you.

Distance matters
03

Shallow Victorian foundations

Terraces and semis from the 1880s to the 1930s often sit on footings far shallower than anything built today, well within the depth at which clay dries out. They have stood for a century, but they respond to dry summers in a way modern foundations do not.

Pre-war stock
04

Old drainage

A cracked Victorian clay drain leaking under a footing can wash out fine material and cause very localised movement. It is one of the more common causes we see, one of the cheaper ones to fix, and it is why a drain survey is a standard early step.

Often the real cause
05

Extensions on newer footings

A 2015 rear extension on modern deep foundations, attached to an 1890s house on shallow ones, will move differently from the house. Cracking at that junction is close to inevitable and is usually differential settlement, not subsidence.

Junction cracking
06

Drier summers

Consecutive dry summers dry clay deeper and for longer than the pattern these houses were built for. That is why subsidence enquiries cluster in late summer and autumn, and why one bad year can surface cracking in a house that never cracked before.

Seasonal peak
Section / 04 Confirming it

How subsidence is actually established.

This is the part that surprises people. Confirming subsidence is an investigation over time, not a diagnosis in an afternoon.

Monitoring across a full year

Crack width gauges or level monitoring, read over six to twelve months, so movement can be seen across a wet and a dry season. Nothing else distinguishes seasonal clay movement from progressive failure, and nothing shortcuts it.

Drain survey

CCTV through the below-ground drainage looking for cracks, displaced joints and leaks near the affected foundation. Cheap relative to everything else, and it resolves a meaningful share of cases outright.

Trial pits

Excavating beside the foundation to see how deep it goes, what it sits on, and what condition the soil is in. This is what turns an opinion about the ground into a fact about the ground.

Soil and root analysis

Samples tested for shrinkage potential and moisture, and any roots found identified by species. Species matters: it establishes which tree is implicated, which decides who is responsible for it.

Nobody can confirm subsidence from a photograph. Anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing, and usually selling something. What photographs are genuinely good for is the opposite job: ruling it out. In the large majority of enquiries we receive, the pattern is plainly shrinkage or thermal movement and we can say so within a day, at no cost, without anybody digging anything up.
Section / 05 Next steps

What to do, in order.

Cheapest and most reversible first. Most people never get past step three.

1. Photograph and date it

Wide shot of the whole wall, close-up of the crack with a coin or ruler for scale, and the same views inside and out where you can. Mark the ends of the crack in pencil. This costs nothing and is the evidence every later step depends on.

2. Get a free read on the photos

Send them to a chartered engineer before spending anything. Most cracks are ruled out at this stage. If yours is one of them, you have your answer and you can stop.

Send photos →

3. Check the obvious causes

Large trees close to the affected wall, gutters or downpipes discharging next to the foundation, a manhole that smells or ponds. These are common, cheap to address, and frequently the whole story.

4. Then, and only then, your insurer

If the pattern genuinely fits subsidence, notify your buildings insurer. Their investigation is at their cost. Understand the trade-offs on premiums and disclosure before you do.

Section / 06 FAQ

Subsidence and settlement FAQs.

If yours is not here, send it with a photo. We read every enquiry.

What is the difference between subsidence and settlement?

Settlement is the building compressing the ground beneath it under its own weight. It is normal, expected in the first few years after construction, and it stops. Subsidence is the ground itself moving downwards and taking the foundation with it, usually because clay has shrunk, tree roots have drawn out moisture or a drain is leaking. Subsidence does not correct itself, and unlike settlement it is covered by most buildings insurance policies.

What do subsidence cracks look like?

Typically diagonal, often stepping through the mortar joints in brickwork, wider at the top than the bottom, visible from both inside and outside, and concentrated around door and window openings. Width above about 3mm is the point at which a crack is worth measuring properly. No single feature is conclusive on its own; it is several appearing together that matters.

How can I tell if a crack is still moving?

Mark each end of the crack in pencil, date it and photograph it, then look again after three months and again after six. Settlement and shrinkage cracks stay where they are. Active movement will have gone past your marks. Formal monitoring uses crack width gauges over a similar or longer period, for the same reason: seasonal ground movement only shows up across a full wet and dry cycle.

Are cracks where my extension joins the house subsidence?

Usually not. An extension normally sits on newer, deeper foundations than the original house, so the two parts move differently and crack along the join. That is differential settlement and it is very common. It is worth a closer look if the crack is wide, tapers noticeably, keeps reopening after filling, or if doors and windows nearby have started sticking.

Does a tree near my house mean subsidence?

Not by itself. Large trees within roughly their own height of a building on clay soil raise the risk, and species such as London plane, poplar, willow and oak draw the most water. Plenty of London houses stand next to mature trees with no movement at all. The tree is one factor investigators weigh alongside the crack pattern, the soil and the drainage, not a diagnosis on its own.

Will my house need underpinning?

Probably not. Underpinning is a last resort and is far less common than homeowners expect. Most confirmed cases are resolved by removing or managing the cause, such as tree works or repairing a leaking drain, then allowing the ground to stabilise before making good the cracking.

Should I tell my insurer before I know what it is?

If the pattern genuinely fits subsidence, yes, and most policies require prompt notification. If it looks like shrinkage or thermal movement, an initial photo review costs nothing and may save you putting an unnecessary enquiry on your record. A recorded subsidence claim has to be disclosed at renewal and when you sell.
