Overview / Crack inspection / Stepped cracks in brickwork
Guide · Written by a chartered structural engineer

What does a stepped crack in brickwork mean?

That one part of the wall has moved relative to the part next to it and that the mortar gave way before the bricks did. That is the normal way for brickwork to crack. On its own it says nothing about how serious the movement was. A stepped crack is a shape, not a diagnosis.

What decides whether it matters is where it is on the building, how wide it is, whether it is wider at one end than the other, whether it is still moving and how old it is. All five are readable from the pavement. Below is how to read them and where stepped cracks usually turn up. After that comes what each place tends to mean and what gets done about the ones that need it.

Section / 01 Why it steps

The staircase shape is the mortar talking, not the bricks.

Brickwork is a hard material held together by a softer one. When the wall is pulled or sheared, the crack takes the easiest route it can find. That route is the joints.

Why does it step?

Mortar is weaker than brick by design, so a crack runs along a bed joint until it reaches the end of a brick, turns up the vertical joint beside it and carries on along the next bed. Repeat that over ten courses and you have a staircase. It is the path of least resistance, nothing more.

Is stepped worse than straight?

Usually the reverse. A crack that has gone straight through the bricks rather than round them means the stress was high enough or arrived fast enough that the joints could not relieve it in time. Or the wall was repointed in hard cement over soft old bricks and the bricks became the weak link.

Does it point at the cause?

As a rule of thumb, yes. A diagonal stepped crack rises towards the part of the wall that has dropped, so the triangle of brickwork it isolates at a corner or above a window is usually the piece that moved. It is a guide to where to look, not a verdict on why.

Old is good. Look inside the crack before you worry about its shape. Paint bridging it, pointing that has been pushed into it, dirt, lichen or weathered brick faces inside it all say the crack has been there for years and has not moved since. Clean, sharp, pale edges say it is recent. A great many stepped cracks in London brickwork are historic and stable. They have been quietly ignored by every owner since the war.
Section / 02 Where it is

Location tells you more than shape.

The same staircase means different things in different places. These are the eight places it usually turns up, in rough order of how often we see them.

Where the house meets an extension

Usually differential settlement and expected. The extension sits on newer, deeper foundations than the house and the two move differently, so the brickwork cracks along the line where they meet. Often vertical, sometimes stepped, rarely wide. It becomes worth a look if it tapers, keeps reopening or is accompanied by doors nearby starting to stick.

Above a window or door

Often the lintel. A stepped crack rising from each top corner of an opening or a triangle of brickwork above it that looks as though it wants to drop, means the support over the opening has moved. In Victorian houses that is frequently a timber lintel rotting behind a brick arch. In newer work it is a concrete or steel lintel deflecting or short on bearing. Local and repairable.

Below a window sill

Read the taper. Brickwork under a window is a short, weak pier between two openings, so it is the first thing to crack when the wall below moves. Stepped cracks running down from a sill towards the ground are common at bay windows, which usually sit on shallower foundations than the rest of the house.

At a corner of the building

The classic picture and the one to take seriously. Diagonal stepped cracks rising towards a corner, isolating a triangle that has dropped, is what foundation movement at that corner looks like. Look for a large tree within its own height of the corner, a drain or gully next to it or a downpipe discharging at its base.

At intervals along a long wall

Usually expansion, not movement. Clay bricks grow slightly for years after they are laid. A long run of brickwork with no movement joint relieves that by cracking, typically vertically or in a shallow step, every 10 metres or so. Garden walls, long flank walls and 1960s–80s houses without joints show it. Cosmetic in most cases.

Straight up, on an outside wall

Vertical is the least alarming direction. A vertical crack through the joints on an external wall, away from any corner or opening, is most often thermal or moisture movement in the brick itself or a junction between two builds. It has no rotation behind it, so it stays the same width top to bottom. Diagonal and tapering is what to look for instead.

Horizontal, along one bed joint

Not a stepped crack at all and worth knowing about. A horizontal crack along a single bed joint, often repeating every six or seven courses, on a cavity wall built after about 1920, is the signature of corroding wall ties. The rusting steel expands and lifts the joint. A wall tie survey is the next step. Not ground movement but not to be left either.

Above a wall that was taken out

The new support is deflecting. Stepped cracking in the brickwork over a knocked-through room, a removed chimney breast or a widened opening means the beam, bracket or lintel carrying that brickwork has moved more than the brickwork can tolerate. Common where work was done without calculations and one of the few stepped cracks where paperwork matters as much as pointing.

Section / 03 Reading it

Five things to read before anyone gets a bill.

None needs equipment beyond a coin, a pencil and a camera. Together they answer most of the question.

01

Width

The industry scale published by the BRE treats anything up to about 1mm as fine and up to about 5mm as slight, both dealt with by repointing. From 5mm to 15mm is moderate and starts to involve replacing bricks. Above 15mm (and certainly above 25mm) the conversation changes regardless of cause. A 10p coin edge is roughly 2mm.

Coin for scale
02

Taper

Wider at the top than the bottom means one side of the wall has rotated downwards, which is what foundation movement does. Wider at the bottom points to heave, ground swelling upwards. The same width along its whole length means no rotation, which is shrinkage or thermal movement. Taper is the single most useful thing to photograph.

Top or bottom
03

Both sides of the wall

Go indoors and look at the plaster in the same place. A crack that shows on both faces has gone through the full thickness of the wall. That moves it from a pointing job to a structural one. A stepped crack on the outside with nothing behind it is often only the outer leaf or the face of the brickwork.

Inside and out
04

Age

Paint or pointing inside the crack, weathering on the broken faces, dirt and growth in the gap or a previous repair that has held all say the movement finished long ago. Fresh, pale, sharp-edged mortar surfaces say it has not. Historic stepped cracks in a Victorian terrace are a feature of the type, not a warning.

Look inside it
05

Whether it is still moving

Pencil a line across the crack at each end, date it and photograph it. Look again in three months and again after a wet winter. A crack that has not passed your marks in six months is, for practical purposes, finished. Formal monitoring with tell-tales does the same job with more precision over a longer period. Nothing shortcuts it.

Time is the instrument
06

The season

London sits largely on clay, which shrinks in a dry summer and swells again over winter. A stepped crack that opens in late summer and partly closes by spring is reading ground moisture. One that appeared once and never changed is not. If yours follows the seasons, our guide on telling subsidence from settlement is the next thing to read.

Section / 04 Next steps

What to do, in order.

Cheapest and most reversible first. The repair depends entirely on the cause, so the cause comes first.

1. Photograph it properly

A wide shot showing the whole wall and where the crack sits on it, a close-up with a coin in the frame and the same spot indoors. Pencil marks across each end, dated. Most of what an engineer needs to say something useful is in those four photographs.

2. Look within a few metres of it

A large tree, a gully or manhole, a downpipe discharging at the base of the wall, a recent trench, a new extension next door. Stepped cracks almost always have their cause nearby and at ground level. Finding it is frequently the whole answer and sometimes the whole repair.

3. Get a free read before spending anything

Send the photographs to a chartered engineer. Old, stable, narrow and untapered cracks get ruled out at this stage and can simply be repointed. If yours is one of them, you have your answer.

Send photos →

4. If it is live or wide, investigate, then repair

Monitoring across a wet and dry season, a CCTV drain survey and (where the pattern genuinely fits ground movement) a conversation with your buildings insurer. Repair follows the cause: repointing for stable hairlines, stainless steel helical bars stitched across the crack every few courses for stable wider ones, a new lintel where the old one has failed, replacement ties for a horizontal crack. Underpinning is the rare last resort, not the default.

Section / 05 FAQ

Stepped crack FAQs.

If yours is not here, send it with a photo. We read every enquiry.

What does a stepped crack in brickwork mean?

It means one part of the wall has moved relative to the part beside it and that the crack followed the mortar joints because mortar is weaker than brick. The staircase shape itself is normal for brickwork and does not indicate how serious the movement was. What matters is where the crack is on the building, how wide it is, whether it is wider at one end, whether it is still moving and how old it is.

Are stepped cracks always subsidence?

No. Most are not. Stepped cracks also come from a lintel failing above a window, differential settlement where an extension meets the house, thermal expansion along a long wall and historic movement that finished decades ago. The pattern that does suggest foundation movement is a diagonal stepped crack rising towards a corner of the building, wider at the top than the bottom, visible inside and out and either recent or seasonal.

Is a stepped crack worse than a straight crack?

Usually not. Stepping through the joints is the low-energy way for brickwork to crack. A crack that runs straight through the bricks themselves means the stress was higher or arrived faster than the joints could relieve or that the wall was repointed in hard cement over soft bricks so the bricks became the weak point. Straight-through cracks are worth a closer look than stepped ones of the same width.

How wide does a crack in brickwork have to be before it is serious?

The industry scale used by surveyors and insurers treats cracks up to about 1mm as fine and up to about 5mm as slight, both normally dealt with by repointing. Between 5mm and 15mm is moderate and usually involves some brickwork being replaced. Above 15mm is severe. Width is only one factor; a 3mm crack that is tapering, recent and visible on both faces of the wall matters more than a 6mm one that was pointed over thirty years ago and has not moved since.

What causes a stepped crack above a window or door?

Most often the lintel. Brickwork over an opening is carried by a lintel or arch. If that support deflects, rots or has too little bearing, the brickwork above it drops slightly and cracks diagonally from the corners of the opening. In Victorian and Edwardian houses this is frequently a timber lintel decaying behind a brick arch. It is a local problem with a local repair and is not a sign of foundation movement on its own.

What does a horizontal crack in brickwork mean?

A horizontal crack running along a single bed joint, particularly one that repeats every six or seven courses on a cavity wall built after about 1920, is the typical sign of corroding cavity wall ties. The steel ties rust, expand and lift the mortar joint they sit in. It is not caused by ground movement. The next step is a wall tie survey. The usual repair is to install replacement ties and isolate the old ones.

How are stepped cracks in brickwork repaired?

It depends on the cause, which is why the cause is established first. A stable hairline crack is simply repointed. A stable wider crack is usually stitched with stainless steel helical bars bedded in grout across the crack every few courses, then repointed. A failed lintel is replaced. Corroded wall ties are replaced. Where the ground is genuinely still moving, the cause is removed or managed (tree works or a drain repair, for example) and the wall is monitored before it is made good. Underpinning is rare.
